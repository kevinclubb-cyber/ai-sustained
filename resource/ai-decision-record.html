The AI Adoption Record

You have handed one job to AI. Is it actually working, and is it steady enough to change how the team is staffed? Five questions, answered in order.

Use one sheet per job. One workflow, one automation, one task. Not one per department, and not one per tool. If AI is drafting your quotes and also sorting your inbox, that is two sheets.

Answer each question with something you have measured. Not something you expect.

01Is it checkable?

Can somebody spot a mistake without doing the whole job again?

Someone has to look over what the AI produced before it goes anywhere. This asks how expensive that look is.

Cheap to check: the AI copies figures off a supplier invoice into your accounts. Your bookkeeper holds the invoice next to the entry and knows in twenty seconds.

Expensive to check: the AI writes a quote for a bespoke job. To know the price is right, somebody has to work the job out themselves. That is not checking, that is doing it twice.

If checking costs about as much as doing, the saving disappears no matter how good the tool is. No is a useful answer. It means pick a different job to automate, not a different AI.

02Is it really saving time?

Run it for a month on real work. Count four things.

Not whether it impresses you. Whether it saves hours once the checking and the fixing are counted too.

A month of ordinary trading, awkward jobs included. Not a demo, not the ten tidy examples you tested it on.

Example: the invoice workflow ran 240 times. On 31 of those the output was wrong enough to start again. Checking took about two minutes each time. Four errors got through and the accountant caught them at month end.

The fourth box is the one everybody forgets. The first three are costs you can see. The fourth is the cost that has quietly landed on somebody else, and it never shows up as an AI problem.

03Who runs it day to day?

Hand it to a junior person, with somebody experienced signing off.

Whoever runs it needs to be able to keep it running. That should not be your most expensive person.

The gain is biggest for the person who had furthest to travel, and it is smallest, sometimes negative, for the person who already did the job well. Handing your best tool to your best person is the instinct, and it is the wrong way round.

Example: the office junior runs the invoice workflow every morning and flags anything odd. The bookkeeper reviews a sample once a week and signs it off. The junior gets better and faster; the bookkeeper keeps the responsibility.

This is also how you find out whether it can survive without you. If only one clever person can keep it working, you have not automated anything. You have created a dependency.

04Is it steady enough to build on?

Work out the real hours saved, before anybody discusses staffing.

This is the only number that should ever reach a staffing conversation, and almost nobody calculates it.

Take the hours the job used to take. Subtract the hours it takes now, the hours spent checking it, and the hours spent fixing what it got wrong. What is left is the real saving.

Example: invoices used to take six hours a week. Now one hour to run, two hours checking, half an hour fixing. Real saving: two and a half hours a week. That is genuinely worth having, and it is not a job.

Never work from a remembered saving. People overestimate what a tool saved them by a wide margin, even straight after being timed doing the opposite. If a box below is blank, you are guessing.

05Would it still hold in two years?

Only if you would still be glad of this decision after two years.

The last question, and the one that stops an expensive mistake.

Assume the tool changes, the supplier changes their invoice layout, and the person who ran it leaves. Would the decision still look right?

Roughly a third of employers who cut a role because of AI have since hired back into it, and the people came back on more money than they left on. Add that cost to your sums before you commit.

If it only works in year one, it does not work. Two and a half hours a week is a real gain. Give those hours back to the person and spend them on something better. That is a perfectly good outcome, and it is the most common one.

A worked example

A twelve-person plumbing firm. AI reads supplier invoices and puts the figures into the accounts spreadsheet. One job, so one sheet.

01  Is it checkable? Yes. The bookkeeper holds the invoice next to the entry. Twenty seconds. Cheap.
02  Really saving time? One month: ran 240 times, started again 31 times, about 2 minutes checking each, 4 errors caught later by the accountant. Handwritten invoices caused most of it.
03  Who runs it? The office junior runs it each morning and flags anything odd. The bookkeeper reviews a sample weekly and signs it off.
04  Real hours saved Used to take 6 hrs a week. Now 1 hr running, 2 hrs checking, 0.5 hrs fixing. Real saving: 2.5 hrs a week.
05  Still hold in two years? Yes, but 2.5 hours is not a job. Decision: keep the role, spend the 2.5 hours chasing late payers.

That is the most common honest outcome. A real saving, given back to the person who was already doing the work.
