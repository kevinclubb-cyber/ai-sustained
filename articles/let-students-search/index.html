Who holds the switch

The search box became a teacher. Nobody interviewed it.

More than 500 Google staff have asked their own bosses to let schools switch off AI answers and keep Search. The row is about who decides what a pupil reads first.

In short: From early 2027, Google is switching off Gemini and Search for school pupil accounts until a school turns them on — but a school that keeps AI off loses Search as well, because, in Google’s words, “Search and AI are deeply integrated.” More than 500 Google staff have signed a petition asking for a switch to turn the AI answers off and keep Search. In England, governors are responsible for filtering, and must review it every year.

An English school cannot put an adult in front of a class without paperwork: proof of identity, references, a DBS check and a line on the single central record. The answer box at the top of a Google search went through none of that. It arrived in the results one day, began explaining photosynthesis and the causes of the First World War to anyone who asked, and has been taking the first question in a great many classrooms since.

This week more than 500 of Google’s own staff signed a petition asking the company to let schools turn that box off. It is called Let Students Search, it was organised by the Alphabet Workers Union, and it is addressed to chief executive Sundar Pichai, Search chief Elizabeth Reid and education chief Chris Phillips. The request is narrow: let a school remove the AI features from Search and keep Search.

Picture the lesson this lands on. Chloe Barnes is one of our composite readers, a second-year English teacher in Lewisham whose Year 10s once handed in six copies of the same essay. Her answer is a unit on weighing sources. Pupils get a question and a page of links, and have to say which they trust and why. It only works if they meet the links before they meet an answer.

What Google actually said.

On 3 November Phillips published a post saying four services would be switched off for school pupil accounts until an administrator turns them on: Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search. Read quickly, that is what campaigners had been asking for. AI stays off unless the school says otherwise.

The trouble was the fourth item. Phillips told the New York Times that a school which keeps AI off loses Search as well, and that its pupils get a notice saying so. His reason fits on a card.

Later that day Google clarified that each product has its own switch and a school may run Search without the Gemini chatbot. What no school can run is Search without AI Overviews and AI Mode, the generated answers that sit above the links. Education Week reports the change starts early in 2027.

Google’s case deserves a fair hearing. It says schools will be able to give pupils a Guided Learning version of Search that walks them through a problem in steps and holds back the answer, and it is piloting that in selected US schools this month. Its post talks about “productive struggle”, which is a serious idea.

The reaction was not gentle. Senator Bernie Sanders called it “extortion”, and former US competition chief Lina Khan accused Google of using its search monopoly to push AI into schools. About 30 parents and educators protested outside Google’s Chicago office. Gizmodo, citing the New York Times, says New York, Los Angeles and Chicago are weighing other search engines.

Scale explains the heat. Google says 150 million students and teachers use Classroom worldwide, which is more than twice the population of the UK. A default chosen in California lands on a lot of desks.

The shortcut became the lesson.

Anyone who taught in the 2000s will enjoy the irony. Search was once the thing schools fretted about. Pupils were told not to trust the first result and never to cite Wikipedia. Two decades on, a plain page of links is what teachers and Google engineers are campaigning to protect, because choosing between sources is the skill and the answer box does the choosing for you.

The evidence is better than the shouting suggests. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 secondary pupils in Turkey, one group practised maths with a plain chatbot and another with a version built to give hints and hold back answers. Both raced ahead in practice. Then the AI was taken away and everyone sat the exam.

So the design decides. And the AI in ordinary Google Search has not been the careful design. When Common Sense Media, a US charity, ran more than 2,600 test searches this summer from accounts set up as 11 and 15 year olds, AI Mode completed all 180 of 180 homework assignments it was given. The charity gave the features its lowest rating. Google rejects the method, saying the test questions were artificial and that it could not reproduce many of the answers.

Follow that through. Guided Learning is Google accepting the teaching argument: pupils learn more when the machine makes them work.

On how children learn, Google and its critics have ended up in the same place. The argument left is who holds the switch.

“Deeply integrated” is a decision.

Nothing in the record so far suggests the separation is beyond Google’s engineers. Fairfax County’s schools in Virginia are testing their own browser extension that hides AI Overviews, and an edtech firm behind a separate public campaign gives one away. Then there is the petition from inside the building, and what its organisers say about whose call this is.

The UK has already made Google build something close. On 3 June the Competition and Markets Authority used its new digital markets powers to order Google to give publishers a way to keep their work out of AI Overviews, which it called a world first, with nine months to comply. Different customer, same lesson: integration bends when someone with authority insists.

A British newspaper has won the right to keep its articles out of Google’s answer box. A British school has no setting that keeps the answer box out of its classrooms.

Why hold the line, then? One theory, put to NPR by a former Google education specialist, is legal: a US judge has accepted Google’s case that Search and its AI are a single product, and a tidy off switch for schools would be inconvenient evidence against that. The competition experts NPR asked were not wholly convinced and did not dismiss it. Google did not answer the question.

So what for a UK school?

Start with the gap in the record. Google has not said whether the new defaults apply outside the United States, and its post names no country. Guided Learning in Classroom is already global, and it was trialled for four weeks in three Ark secondary schools here before launch. A UK school on Google Workspace should assume the choice is coming and ask its reseller now.

Then look at who carries the duty. In England, Keeping Children Safe in Education 2026 has been in force since 1 September and expects filtering and monitoring to be reviewed at least once every academic year. The DfE standard behind it gives governing bodies overall strategic responsibility and counts AI-generated content as something filtering has to handle. So a governing body answers for what a search box tells a 12-year-old, while the setting that controls it sits with the supplier. (That guidance covers England. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland set their own.)

The same guidance made English schools mobile phone-free by default this year, so the Department for Education is plainly willing to set a default when it thinks one matters. On search, the default currently arrives with the product.

Meanwhile the pupils are well ahead of the policy. The National Literacy Trust’s 2026 survey put the question to more than 40,000 young people.

The staffroom has moved as well. The same survey asked 2,567 teachers, and two things rose together: their own use of AI, and their worry about what pupils do with it.

Nor is the British state a bystander. The DfE wants AI tutoring tools in schools by the end of 2027, reaching up to 450,000 disadvantaged pupils. Google was on the first supplier list and is no longer on it. Neither side has explained why. The government said only that it would not give one supplier different terms from the rest.

The uncomfortable part for both camps.

If you want AI out of schools, the switch will not deliver that. Roughly four in five teenagers have already used it. Copying its answers is more common among pupils on free school meals, and only 31% of the lowest-income parents feel confident helping with AI, against 80% of the highest-income. For some children, school is the one place they will meet either a guided tool or an adult with time to explain. An all-or-nothing setting takes away the option that would serve them best, which is links first and AI when the teacher says so.

For those who want AI everywhere, the universities offer a warning. On 11 November England’s higher education regulator reported that detection tools do not reliably catch AI use, and said it will expect assessment that is secure, which in many cases means AI-free. A 2026 survey by HEPI and Kortext puts AI use in assessed work at 94% of UK undergraduates. If degrees are heading back to the exam hall, schools are where unassisted thinking has to be built first.

Which brings us back to Chloe’s Year 10s. Her unit needs control at the level of a lesson: AI answers off while pupils weigh sources, a guided tool on for revision. Under Google’s plan a school can set different rules for different groups of pupils, but any group barred from AI loses Search with it. Her choice would be the answer box on top of every search, or no Google Search in the room.

Schools interview everyone else who takes questions from children. The least a supplier can offer is the right to say not yet.

Tactical takeaway

Find out who set the default, then set it yourself.

01 · In the classroom
Put the answer box on trial. Give pupils one AI answer and the sources beneath it, and have them mark the machine’s homework.
02 · At home
Ask the school one question: what sits at the top of a search on a school login, and who chose it? A shrug is an answer too.
03 · For governors
Put search AI on this year’s filtering review. Ask the supplier in writing whether AI Overviews can be suppressed, and minute who set the default.
